I wasn't looking for a pattern, but I have noticed that many of the bands I've liked over the past year or two are based in the Yangtze River Delta (长三角): Shanghai and its surroundings. It's also called Jiang-Zhe-Hu (江浙沪) by natives, meaning Jiangsu, Zhejiang and Shanghai. A network of independent music spread across several cities is an appealing subject, but too broad to take on in one go. So let us start smaller, with a few of the conditions that shape the present scene.

Seen from today's independent scene, the Delta's chief asset is how easy it is to get around. Shanghai, Hangzhou, Suzhou, Ningbo, Nanjing: the big cities of the lower Yangtze are an hour or two apart by high-speed rail, which makes them day-trip distance, tiredness the next morning aside. I find distances in China hard to judge, but the attitude seems to be: shall we just meet in Shanghai, then? In Tokyo terms, Shinjuku.

Rock in China is generally dated to 1986, but records of Shanghai from that time are scarce and depend largely on the recollections of those involved. With that in mind, let's begin in the 1990s.

## Underground Shanghai 2000

Underground Shanghai 2000, released on 30 June 2000, is a compilation that encases a moment in Shanghai's underground rock scene at the turn of the century in amber. Ten bands then active in the city appear on it: Godot (戈多), Bird on a Wire  (惊弓之鸟), The Nutcrackers (胡桃夹子), Bug, Seven, Sankufar, Dead Poet Society (死亡诗社), Wutang (无糖, "No Sugar"), Loud Speaker (扩音器) and Prague Spring (布拉格之春). The styles are far from uniform, running through punk, metal, post-punk, psychedelia and slowcore. What the bands shared was that they sang almost entirely in Chinese, and that they were doggedly independent.

The 2023 reissue adds a second disc to the remastered original, featuring tracks by several bands who shared stages with them at the time. The two discs give a broader picture of Shanghai's underground rock scene in those days. It also comes with a booklet of recollections from those involved and photographs from the period, apparently. I haven't got hold of a copy yet, so I'm left to imagine the rest from scraps on the web. The music can be heard in full on YouTube at the moment, and I'd recommend doing so sooner rather than later.

Now, let's look at how this compilation came about and how Shanghai's early rock scene took shape.

## Rock in 1990s Shanghai

Reform and opening-up, along with a policy of concentrating industry in the capital, spilled over into popular music and left Beijing's standing as the centre of rock unassailable. Hard rock flourished, the "Three Heroes of Magic Stone" (魔岩三杰) were pushed hard onto the market, and the original premise, rebellion against the mainstream, was often left hanging. The rest of the country was largely overlooked, though each region was nurturing rock of its own and biding its time.

At the end of 1997, a rock festival for local Shanghai bands was held, known as the Shanghai Rock Convention (上海摇滚大会). Ten bands played to an audience of around a thousand. The venue was the Shanghai Youth Cultural Activity Centre (上海青年文化活动中心), which had opened that same year, a facility run directly by the Shanghai committee of the Communist Youth League and devoted, by its own account, to "serving the healthy growth and personal development of young people".

The organiser was Sun Mengjin (孙孟晋), who hosted a rock programme on a local radio station. He also played in the show, as the vocalist of Godot, and hoisted the No Wave flag over Shanghai rock. A post-punk-minded underground took shape in the city around the time Nanjing's P.K.14 formed, or perhaps slightly before. Seven, named after the Hollywood film starring Brad Pitt, showed a pronounced Joy Division influence. Bird on a Wire, who started out in a university art studio, took Television and The Cure as models and spoke for young people living in a modern city. Who deserves the credit for China's first post-punk band is a matter of some debate among historians, though I don't think it matters who.

## How the Word Got Around

Shanghai had the country's most developed telephone network, yet until the mid-1990s few individuals could get a landline, never mind a mobile. The internet was a long way from everyday life. Before P2P software, let alone subscription services, info about rock spread mainly through music magazines and personal networks.

In that pre-internet world, rock fans got to know one another at events run by the Audio-Visual World fan club, at makeshift gig venues, in instrument shops, in university clubs, or in dim back rooms that dealt in "dakou" (打口). This kind of music was obscure enough that turning up in the right place at the right time gave you better than random odds of making new friends.

### Audio-Visual World

In October 1987, China Record Corporation (中国唱片), a major music publisher based in Beijing, launched Audio-Visual World (音像世界, Yinxiang Shijie), a general entertainment magazine for the new era. It was run almost entirely from Shanghai. The Shanghai branch couldn't obtain the licence it needed to publish, so the magazine appeared under the head office's name in Beijing. Shanghai was the most open and developed city in the country, and the cutting edge of culture. To a Shanghainese of the time, Shenzhen was still little more than a fishing village waiting to be developed. In a world without the internet or streaming, an ambitious magazine covering pop from Taiwan and Hong Kong as well as the emerging pop of the mainland had the wind behind it and led the industry. It was nearly the only official window in the country onto foreign music.

In 1989 the magazine's official fan club, the Audio-Visual World Fan Club (音像世界歌迷会), was founded, one of the first of its kind in China. Screening videos for members was one of its main activities: someone would get hold of foreign rock and pop videos and documentaries by one means or another, and everyone watched them together. Pink Floyd's The Wall was shown at least once, which may be one of the origins of prog's popularity in China. The club also put on concerts, and the newsletter produced for each event often ran pieces by members as well as by the editorial staff.

Magazine distribution in China was handled by the postal bureau, which seems to have been less than helpful towards an oddball newcomer. Club members therefore doubled as sales reps, pushing the magazine to retailers. With devoted readers behind it and no serious rival, circulation shot up, and is said to have passed 200,000 comfortably by the mid-1990s.

### Radio on Air

Radio is the other thing many Shanghainese remember from the period. Sun Mengjin of Godot discovered Western rock as a student and was hooked from then on. After graduating he took a job as a museum curator, and in 1993, through an acquaintance, landed a slot as a host at the local radio station. The station had only been founded in 1992, during a reform of Shanghai's broadcasting policy, and ran a 24-hour music channel. He was given a Wednesday slot for a programme introducing Western pop. The first episode was on the Beatles and Bob Dylan, where his own listening had begun. Presumably his record collection qualified him for the job. In 1995 his employers found out about the other job. The reason for his weekly late arrivals came out, and before long he swapped his museum post for the rather flimsy title of radio DJ.

Sun and his programme stayed on air through several reshuffles and renamings. It was a little different from an ordinary music show: If Sun remembers correctly, Keiji Haino got played, poetry got read. His Shanghai-accented Mandarin and highly personal delivery spread the idea of the alternative among young Shanghainese, and the kids glued to their radios at the time grew up to be writers, artists and ordinary people who stood up for culture. Sun himself seems to have turned his radio track record into capital, and started down the road to criticism.

### Two Modern Variations

In June 1999, after the radio show Modern Variations (现代变奏), hosted by Sun Mengjin, came to an end, Zhang Zhiqiang (章志强), a founding member of Bird on a Wire, set up a personal website also called Modern Variations. At first it consisted mainly of articles reposted from music magazines, but a chat room was soon added, and it became a gathering place where band people and local rock fans met night after night. This was the era of dial-up. The site gradually became known, mostly within rock circles, through plugs on Sun's new radio show and announcements at gigs. What began as a place for idle chat soon acquired topic-based BBS sections, and grew into a social hub that fostered a community around alternative culture in Shanghai.

### Physical Records

Like the rest of China in those days, Shanghai depended almost entirely on dakou (打口) for access to foreign music. From the 1990s, surplus stock from record companies in the West was meant for destruction, and was shipped to China instead as recycled raw material. After passing through several middlemen, they reached consumers hungry for foreign music. It was certainly a grey market, hard to call official distribution. There was something slightly shady about it, but the goods themselves came from legitimate sources. Raids were frequent, yet trade was conducted half in the open and became one of the signature industries of the age.

According to some musicians of the time, young players tended to listen to a single album again and again, possibly because of something in the Shanghainese temperament. Recordings were supplied in countless numbers, but none of them was complete. To prove it was waste, every dakou disc had been damaged in some way, and most could not be played to the end. The only other reference material consisted of foreign instruction books, translated by volunteers without the permission of the copyright holders, and a limited number of photocopied scores, so what information there was came in scraps. The listener's imagination had to supply the whole shape of a performance, and its details. The result was an accidental freedom to reinterpret, which the young people of the time had no choice but to exercise, and which they used to hone styles and a vocabulary of their own. It may even be that this allowed loyalty to foreign acts and styles to sit easily alongside a distinctiveness of their own, shaped by the time and place they inhabited.

## Making Do

People who took up rock found a voice that suited them in Western music. They used its methods to express themselves. Music was not a route to success but a thoroughly useless hobby. In that sense, most bands of the time were rough and, at the same time, sincere. Nobody talked big about making rock a career or playing at the Budokan. The greatest success they could realistically picture was becoming the regular house band at some bar.

### Venues and Rehearsal Rooms

In 1996, with foreign investment pouring into Shanghai, a Hard Rock Cafe opened. It was the second in China, after the Beijing branch. With prices that were outrageous for the time, it is hard to see it as a place where Shanghainese met Western pop culture in daily life. Put plainly, it was a theme park aimed mainly at foreigners living in China. The bands playing there were Filipino, rather than local.

Confusingly, a bar called Hard Rock 酒吧 (jiuba means "bar") had already opened in Shanghai in 1993. Since rock arrived in China, its main stage for rock bands had been public spaces rented for a day. Beginning with Buluoren Bar (部落人酒吧) in 1992, rock bars with permanent performance space began to support underground players, and Hard Rock Bar was one of them. Together with Harley's Bar (哈雷酒吧), which opened in 1996, these three were the local rock scene's headquarters in the 1990s. Dedicated live houses had to wait for the twenty-first century.

As the state of its venues suggests, rock in China's second city has never been in good health since the 1980s, and was chronically short of facilities. Even in the late 1990s Shanghai had no commercial studio that amateur bands could easily use. In crowded cities, playing loud is among the troubles musicians commonly run into. Most bands spent their time worrying about where to rehearse.

### Campus and Beyond the Gates

Students were better off in this respect. Universities, official or otherwise, had places to play live all over their grounds, and could host rehearsals and concerts of various sizes. The atmosphere was a little freer than on the street, and campus newsstands carried the many music magazines launched through the 1990s. Outside the gates, dakou sellers waited for sound-starved young people, and instrument and audio shop owners liked to open near universities. Rock was young people's music.

But students graduate after a set period of study. Campus facilities are for those who come after, and working adults who wanted to carry on with a band were back to worrying about where to play. A place with the three things rock requires was not easy to find. Cheap rent, unlimited volume and easy access. Most solutions ended up being empty warehouses, disused factories and underground air-raid shelters.

### Underground Air-Raid Shelters

In the 1960s, as tensions with the Soviet Union rose, underground shelters were built all over China, and Shanghai was no exception. By the 1990s the threat of military conflict had receded and the full extent of the shelters had already been forgotten. Nobody paid any attention to what they had been built for. Some became storerooms, some factories, some dormitories, as landlords and tenants saw fit.

The shelter under Pingliang Road in Yangpu District was far bigger than any other. A Cold War relic, built deep underground in solid concrete to withstand a nuclear attack, it is said to have given anyone who walked in a solemn, unreal feeling. Most importantly, in an underground bunker you didn't have to worry about friction with people living above. You could make as much noise as you liked. Between 1996 and 1997 many bands made this cellar their base. A band called Qianxian (前线) found it first, followed by a dozen or so others, Godot and Bird on a Wire among them. A kingdom of rock, underground.

As ever, there was a catch. The damp of a cellar dug into low-lying ground was far from ideal for electrical equipment, and the frequent comings and goings of oddly dressed men and women were enough to earn the landlord's disapproval. Contracts weren't renewed, and within a year most bands were wandering the city. The same scene played out in air-raid shelters all over town. A band finds a good place, invites friends, annoys the neighbours, then it is time to move. To ordinary people, rock bands were still bizarre and disreputable.

### Disused Factories and Empty Warehouses

In 1999, members of the metal band Heise Shandian (黑色闪电) teamed up with an acquaintance who ran an instrument shop and opened the Red Wing Music Factory (红翅膀音乐工场) near Wujiaochang in Yangpu. In a converted building on the grounds of a scrap factory, it offered instrument lessons and rehearsal space, and held gigs at weekends billed as parties. It deserves a mention that these finished at 5 p.m. so as not to disturb the neighbours, which is a wholesome way to run a rock venue. After about a year the operators fell out, and from 2000 the sign read Gua 2 Music Factory (挂2音乐工场), but it remained an important base for Shanghai's underground rock. According to people who were there, the large room used for gigs had no windows or air conditioning, and in summer the heat often stopped play. The sound, on top of that, is said to have been unspeakably rubbish.

Another rehearsal space was on the site of a disused factory. Named after its street, Songshan Road Rehearsal Room (嵩山路排练室). Godot had been turfed out of the shelter the year before. Their guitarist, Wu Jun (吴峻), attracted by the short commute and the savings, converted the first floor above ground level of a building on the site into a rehearsal room and moved in. When Godot weren't rehearsing, it was lent to other bands, and it became a hangout for the scene. And not only for band people: self-declared Godot fans and friends of friends who had heard about the place on the Modern Variations BBS were constantly dropping in. Wu would often come home from work to find his room full of strangers. A restaurant occupied the ground floor, and at mealtimes the dishes everyone had ordered, at five yuan each, were laid out on the table like a lavish banquet. It was an episode from a temporary utopia that appeared as the city reorganised itself. The rehearsal room ran for a year and was closed when the district was redeveloped. Time to move again.

The rehearsal space with the oddest origins was a middle-school storeroom. Zhang Zhiqiang of Bird on a Wire, who graduated from East China University of Science and Technology in 1998, went to work for an audio equipment company. One day he visited a middle school on the edge of the city to install a sound system. In conversation, the headmaster complained that the school's remote location left the teachers with little in the way of cultural entertainment. Zhang suggested the teachers form a band. The logic is unclear, but the result was that a full set of band equipment was invoiced as a sale, and a storeroom on the school grounds was converted into a rehearsal room. A bit plain to call a studio, but it also happened to solve Bird on a Wire's own rehearsal problem.

## Making Underground Shanghai 2000

The compilation I mentioned at the start was planned and produced by Wu Jianjing (吴建京), frontman of Bird on a Wire. With graduation approaching, he came up with the idea in May of documenting the bands around him. A month before the album's release. This is not a misprint. Wu knew nothing about making records at the time, so the process was very simple. He asked bands he knew to hand in a few tracks. If a band already had recordings, as Seven did, he used those as they were. If there was nothing yet, they were to go and record something somewhere. The first six tracks on the album were recorded in the middle-school storeroom. Zhang Zhiqiang took a microphone and mixer from work, and even supplied a computer and recording software. Wu chose the tracks. One Prague Spring's song ran to over thirty minutes and was cut to about seven, though no band complained.

The money was raised by borrowing from all sorts of people. The plan was a run of 300, but a total budget of 3,000 yuan was a little tight for pressed CDs. The solution? CD-Rs. At the time the fastest optical drives could burn at 4x, and common ones managed 2x. Burning a 15-track, 70-minute audio CD was no quick job. Wu asked his friends for help, and each made between 20 and 50 copies. The last discs were finished only a few days before the release. It pays to have friends.

The sleeve was printed the night before the release at the design firm where a friend who designed the cover art worked. The ink ran out partway through, and the rest was done in black and white. Whether or not it was connected, the friend left the firm soon afterwards. Packing went on after the printing until dawn on the day of the launch.

A launch show was planned to coincide with the release. On the morning of the concert, Wu went to the student hall at East China University of Science and Technology, where the show was to be held, and was told that permission had been withdrawn. The reason given was "safety issues". Shows had been held at the venue several times before, but force majeure turns up in every era and can be stretched to cover almost anything. Notices and publicity had already gone out, and it was too late to cancel. He set about looking for another venue on campus, thinking that in the worst case any classroom would do. Chenyuan (晨园) was a hotel at the western edge of the campus, normally used to host visitors from outside the university. When Wu and the others went to ask about holding a rock concert there, the middle-aged owner said something along the lines of "young people should do what they want to do", and readily agreed to lend them the banqueting hall.

The show got going at around seven in the evening. Admission was free, and there was free beer as well. Most of the bands on the compilation took the stage, including Victoria Space (维多利亚空间), who hadn't made it onto the compilation. The crowd was made up of rock kids from all over Shanghai, plus a few students from East China University of Science and Technology who had been drawn in by the noise. The equipment was, for the most part, whatever Bird on a Wire used for rehearsals. The stage was too small, so the instruments simply sat on the floor. Perhaps the sound had something to do with it too. Still, no stage is sometimes the best stage.

The show carried on until late at night. Afterwards the members repaired to the Sichuan restaurant opposite the school gates, took over the entire ground floor and drank until the owner rang the police. Turfed out, they were still in high spirits and moved on to the street, where they proceeded to turn the neighbourhood upside down.
