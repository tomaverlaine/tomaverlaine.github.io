Day by day, the amount of information around us keeps increasing, and yet somehow there are fewer and fewer things that truly touch our hearts. In our busy everyday lives, we often push our own feelings aside. Have you ever had a moment when you tried to look away from that faint ache deep inside your chest? When that happens, maybe music can help fill that vooid. At least, that's how I feel.

So this time, I thought I’d introduce seven Chinese emo bands to listen to when you’re feeling a bit off.

---

### Chinese Football (Wuhan)

The undisputed King of Chinese Emo. Needs no introduction.

(Introduction omitted. See below.)

**Recommended tracks:** "Electric Girl (电动少女)", "Amusement Park Life (人生游乐场)"

### v是兔子 Wishtoday (Zhengzhou)

**Stirring up chaos at the forefront of Chinese indie.**

I'll be honest. The first time I heard them, that raw, exposed sound startled me. Stay with that startle for a moment.

"Lo-fi" doesn't say enough. It's as if the urgency has become sound just as it is. No pretense in the emotion, no escape. It hits you right in the chest.

**Recommended tracks:** "Gloomy Night and Some Swirling Duet Dances (阴郁的夜晩以及某些回旋不止的双人舞)", "等待d"

### Silly Function (Wuhan)

**Down-to-earth rock at full pelt.**

If you hear they're Chinese Football's younger peers, a few ears might prick up. Notice that. If anything, though, this is fairly straight-ahead rock — catchy riffs, crisp dynamics, melodies you'll want to hum along to — with the low-key frustration and bitterness of everyday life carefully ladled in. The kind of band that gets you moving before you know it.

**Recommended tracks:** "My New World Never Unfolds Because of Anyone Else (我的新世界从不因他人而展开)", "Commuter Battle Royale (通勤大逃杀)"

### Nein or Gas Mus (Guangzhou)

**The cornerstone of Guangzhou twinkle emo.**

I'll admit I got properly stuck just trying to work out how to read the band name the first time. A little behind Chinese Football chronologically, but a band with genuine staying power — tricky Midwest-derived musicianship with cynical lyrics riding on top, and somehow you can't quite bring yourself to dislike them. They've kept this going for over a decade, squeezed in around actual day jobs, which is, well...

**Recommended tracks:** "1st", "Ink"

### Diels-Alder (Lanzhou)

**The name, apparently, refers to an organic chemistry reaction — an alkene adding to a conjugated diene to form a six-membered ring. A bit of sciency.**

Their playing and vocals are powerful, striking, recalling 2000s emo rock — and yet the delicate tapping running underneath keeps it from ever settling into nostalgia.

**Recommended tracks:** "Jet Lag and Flat White", "Super Fuzz Boy (超級法兹男孩)"

### 中西部情绪麻将 Midwest Emo Mahjong (Guangzhou)

**New-generation emo. I heard they formed while playing mahjong in Guangzhou's Midwest district. That's the story. I'm not fact-checking it.**

The melodies kind of float and feel disconnected at the same time, and they sing them in this flat, deadpan way, which weirdly makes it hit you even harder. I don't even know if they're into Japanese rock or not, but there's something familiar and nostalgic about it. The drummer apparently plays in some other bands.

**Recommended tracks:** "I'm Not from the Midwest", "Always Sad"

### 郁云 Emo Cloud (Qingdao)

**Qingdao, Shandong — known for its beer.**

Breakbeat-style polyrhythms link up with post-hardcore passion, holding the urgency all the way through without ever quite landing on catharsis. That suspended feeling matches the mood some days. More or less.

**Recommended tracks:** "12", "It's Raining All Day in My Heart"

---

### In closing: nothing's really going to change, but

Listening to these definitely isn't going to dramatically transform your life starting tomorrow.

But there's something in the fact that, somewhere out there, someone is carrying around the same kind of grittiness and getting on with it regardless. Isn't there something meaningful in noticing that?

Do give it a go. Just turn the volume up a notch.

## A Tentative Essay on Chinese Emo, or, Nostalgia for Sincerity and Purity

In recent years, Chinese indie rock has been so thoroughly overrun by emo that people have taken to calling it "the Age of Emo". The history of Chinese emo began in the mid-2000s as an import of the overseas wave, and took shape at the intersection of several musical lineages and contexts, threaded together by a DIY network built through exchange across East Asia. Let's retrace this phenomenon from its origins, then, and work out why — why here, why now, and why emo.

#### The Soil of Chinese Emo and the Rise of Post-Rock

**The Reception of Emo and the Formation of a DIY Scene**

During the mid-2000s, the overseas emo wave arrived in China in more or less real time. It became an entry point for punks and metalheads casting around for a new style, with Beijing's Tookoo among the first.

From Guangzhou, known as the "Hardcore Capital of China", bands like 车头灯 (Headlights), CO2 and 大话@梅 picked up a certain profile and helped energise the underground scene.

In 2016, Guangzhou's DIY label Qiii Snacks Records (琪琪音像) invited Taiwan's Touming Magazine (透明雜誌) to come over. Working with Hong Kong's Sweaty & Cramped — a label Qiii Snacks had long been in touch with — they put on a joint tour with Hong Kong's Emptybottles. The Taiwanese emo quartet had built up a strong following in Guangzhou, and apparently left a deep impression on audiences across the region — not just through the music itself, but through a hardcore ethic that put artistic independence above everything else. Though it's probably fairer to see this less as a shared ideology and more as what happened to connect the musicians at the centre of the scene.

**The Rise of Post-Rock and Math Rock**

In 1999, Dou Wei (窦唯), one of Chinese rock's pioneers, covered a song by Bark Psychosis. That cover is generally credited with marking the dawn of post-rock in China. Around this time, among imported CDs, Japanese pressings had an established reputation for sound quality and sold briskly. Releases on the Japanese label Headz, thanks to the label's careful curation, served as a compass for the genre.

Bands from elsewhere made their way into the field. To name a couple: Guangzhou's Zhaoze (沼泽), who started out in grunge/punk, and, in Shanghai, Bragg (布拉格) and Godot (戈多) — bands with clear roots in post-punk, both raised in the underground avant-garde scene — switched over to post-rock.

In 2006, the dedicated post-rock label 1724 Records was founded in Beijing. Bands like Sparrow (文雀) and The Grinding Ear (逆耳) started out, and this was also the period when Dalian's Wang Wen (惘闻), who later became one of the most representative acts of Chinese post-rock, gained a reputation.

In September 2009, China's first post-rock festival was held in Guangzhou, by which point the post-rock boom was obvious to anyone paying attention. This kicked off a series of post-rock festivals around the country. In 2011, Envy from Japan played their first shows in Beijing and Shanghai, showing how much demand there was. Into this post-rock current, a math-rock strain flowed, and Japanese bands played no small role here.

**The Influence of Japanese Math Rock**

Toe's 2005 album *The Book About My Idle Plot on a Vague Anxiety* stunned listeners with its rhythmic construction and the technical ability it demanded. It's still regarded as something close to scripture. Lite have toured China regularly since their first visit in 2016, and reportedly drew an online audience of roughly 100,000 for a livestreamed show in 2020 — that's the scale of their popularity there. Tricot, with their catchy melodic lines, have brought the appeal of math rock to a far broader crowd, and have shared bills with Chinese Football more than once. More recently, the pop-leaning Suichu Spica (水中スピカ) seem to be doing well for themselves too.

During the latter half of the 2010s, Japanese acts frequently toured China, and emo bands were no exception. Many bands worked on a DIY basis, without going through agencies, and still managed to play for Chinese fans. What's more, it was not uncommon for their live audiences in China to be bigger than those back in Japan.

#### The Arrival of Chinese Football and the Formation of an East Asian Network

**Chinese Football, "The King of Chinese Emo": **

Chinese Football, based in Wuhan, affectionately nicknamed "Guozu (国足)" (national football team) by fans, formed in 2011 and has been one of the bands leading Chinese indie rock ever since. That the name is a pun on American Football, the flagship band of a genre called Midwest Emo, hardly needs saying. To begin with, the group was born as an April Fools' joke project, and it has since dragged on by inertia to this day.

Their motto, "Break out of Asia, march onto the world stage," is apparently lifted wholesale from the Chinese national football team's slogan. For all its gung-ho rhetoric, we can safely call this self-deprecating humour: it maps the national team's modest standing in the world directly onto the current state of Chinese rock.

While acknowledging a deep debt to Midwest emo and math rock, the band has wide-ranging roots: hardcore in the tradition of Envy and Fugazi, '90s Japanese rock, and American indie.

Their 2015 debut album made waves even in Japan, thanks to a band name that was frankly a bit too on-the-nose, paired with a manga-style cover illustration. It came out on Wild Records (野生唱片), a local independent label co-run by Chinese Football frontman Xubo (徐波), and was the label's very first release. The label itself grew out of Vox Livehouse, a sacred site for Wuhan punk. So the "King of Emo" also turns out to be the legitimate heir to Wuhan, the "Punk Capital".

**Xubo and the DIY Lineage**

Xubo has been running the solo project A Hidden Trace since the 2000s, and its DIY aesthetic sits comfortably alongside the emo revival of the same period. Though which came first, chicken or egg, is up for debate. He also appeared on *Our Secret World*, the 2015 Sarah Records tribute compilation put together by Boring Productions (无聊制作). It proves that his range isn't confined to emo at all. He's also known for actively introducing city pop to the Chinese indie world. How a man of eclectic taste. Adding to this, he posts constantly on social media about how much he dotes on his dog.

After the band toured Japan and Xubo deepened his ties with its emo scene, he founded Sango Records (珊瑚唱片), a DIY label operating out of Wuhan and Kyoto that distributes indie releases from both countries. The name presumably evokes coral quietly building bridges beneath the waterline between Japan and China.

#### The East Asian Emo Alliance

After pulling off the Touming Magazine–Emptybottles tour, Sweaty & Cramped and Qiii Snacks Records teamed up again in 2016 for the emo compilation *Emotion, No*. It featured bands from China, Japan, Singapore, Hong Kong, and Indonesia, a visible sign of the East Asian emo scene's widening points of contact. A 2018 sequel included VOOID, led by former Touming Magazine frontman Hung Shenhao (洪申豪).

**Parannoul and Lockdown**

*To See the Next Part of the Dream*, the 2021 second album from Parannoul, a shoegaze project of a South Korean student, drew attention from around the world. Naturally, the aftershock reached China too. Via Rate Your Music, one assumes.

Parannoul's humble bedroom setup — just a laptop and a phone — and a sound that fuses a whole range of styles into something chaotic made them a role model for Generation Z, who had no choice but to live through the COVID-19 lockdown era. In a sense, this rewrites the anecdote about Carsick Cars' Zhang Shouwang (张守望) teaching himself guitar out of boredom during the SARS quarantine. It's worth noting, at least, that social crisis and personal isolation keep resurfacing as pivotal moments in indie music history.

Shunji Iwai has enjoyed enormous popularity in China since the 1990s, and a sensibility built around "youth, pain, and loss" is widely shared as a result. Parannoul's nod to *All About Lily Chou-Chou* must have been exactly the interpretive key to that otherwise baffling musicality.

#### Emotion as the Undercurrent of Chinese Indie

Qibadian (七八点), active in Nanjing in the 1990s, and Nezha (哪吒), in Beijing in the 2000s — two bands separated by time and place, both extremely short-lived. Each left behind a single album and achieved no success whatsoever while active; those albums appeared only after the bands had broken up. Devoted listeners kept their music alive long after they were gone, and today they are near-mythical figures.

Their style is usually filed under post-punk or at least indie rock, but many of today's emo bands cite them as an influence, and that cannot be ignored. More than twenty years ago, they sang about the impatience and claustrophobia of people at the mercy of economic growth, urban restructuring, and social change. Because that voice was recorded with plain playing and poor sound quality, it still sounds raw and urgent and has outlasted its own era. Nostalgia for purity and sincerity remains a deep undercurrent in Chinese indie rock, and "emo" is now the word that carries it.

Calling Chinese Football "young" today may be a stretch, but their attitude has not changed since their early days. To sum up emo in rock today, nothing says it better than their own words:

> Every song is a retelling of the same story: an illusion, and its collapse. Music that is simple, complex, smooth, and clumsy, all at once — overflowing with words (evaporating), dreams (crumbling), passion (not yet cooled), and screams (timidly).
